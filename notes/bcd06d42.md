A rotor $R$ about the origin, and a translator $T_C$ by the position of a
point $C$, combine into a rotation about the axis through $C$:

```typst
M = T_C R T_C^(-1).
```

That conjugation is the general pattern for turning an origin-centred motion
into a motion about another frame, and it is the same formula as a change of
basis in linear algebra. The result is a motor with both rotational and
translational parts.

```ocaml
open Geom
let v3 x y z = Nx.create Nx.float32 [| 3 |] [| x; y; z |] in
let sc x = Nx.scalar Nx.float32 x in

let about_point ~c ~axis ~angle =
  let t = Motor.translate c in
  let r = Motor.rotate ~axis ~angle in
  Motor.mul t (Motor.mul r (Motor.inverse t))

let m = about_point ~c:(v3 1. 0. 0.) ~axis:(v3 0. 0. 1.)
                  ~angle:(sc (Float.pi /. 2.)) in
Nx.to_array (Cga.coords (Cga.apply_motor m (Cga.point3 2. 0. 0.)))
(* [|1.; 1.; 0.|]: the point swings round the centre at (1,0,0) *)
```

In a kinematic chain the same idea appears as the *fixed* transform between link
frames, and each joint is either a rotor about its own local axis or a
translator along it ([[23f492c8|Forward kinematics is a motor product]]). A screw joint is the general case: the
exponential of a twist that has both parts ([[6132d759|The SE(3) exponential turns a twist into a screw]]).

Note that the order of the product is the matrix order ([[5b1746c2|Motors compose like matrices]]),
and that conjugation preserves the versor structure, so the result is a motor of
the same norm; no renormalisation is needed for a single composition, though
long products should call `Motor.normalize` ([[1d3a2467|Numerical cautions in a projective algebra]]).
