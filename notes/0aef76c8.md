Classical engineering geometry is a pile of unrelated operations. The dot
product measures alignment. The cross product measures perpendicularity but
only in three dimensions, and it hands back a normal vector where the honest
answer is a plane. Quaternions rotate. Homogeneous matrices translate. Plücker
coordinates describe lines, in a formalism with a constraint nobody remembers.
Each tool has its own composition rule, its own interpolation, its own
singularities, and its own trick for differentiating through it.

Geometric algebra replaces the pile with one associative product on one vector
space. Given two vectors, [[b65e7fa9|the geometric product]] $u v$
splits into a symmetric part, the metric, and an antisymmetric part, the
[[8e33745c|wedge]] $u and v$. Every classical operation is a
[[ad136f1c|grade selection]] of that product or a
[[dc7fd3f4|sandwich]] by a product of vectors. A plane is a first-class object
(the bivector) rather than the shadow of a normal, so the same formula works in
two, three and five dimensions.

```ocaml
open Geom

let u = E3.vector3 1. 0. 0. in
let v = E3.vector3 0. 1. 0. in
E3.scalar_prod u v   (* 0. : they are perpendicular *)
E3.op u v            (* e12: the plane they span *)
E3.gp u v            (* e12: the product, which here has no scalar part *)
```

Two consequences matter for software. Predicates become
[[5080aa18|polynomial]], hence differentiable and vectorisable.
And objects and motions live in the same algebra, so one
[[dc7fd3f4|The sandwich is an algebra automorphism]] transforms points, spheres, planes and lines without a case
analysis; [[be8615ea|Three models, one core]] is the menu of spaces where that pays off.

The honest counterweight is [[8de937ed|the comparison with matrices]]: for
poses alone, dual quaternions are exactly this, and for pure linear algebra a
matrix is fine. The gain is in mixing geometry with kinematics, which is
[[39201d29|precisely the robotics case]].
