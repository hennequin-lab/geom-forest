The geometric product is the Clifford product: associative, distributive,
bilinear, and non-commutative. For two vectors it splits cleanly,

```typst
u v = u dot v + u and v,
```

into a scalar and a bivector. The scalar part is how much the vectors agree.
The bivector part is how much they disagree, and it records the plane. For
general elements the product mixes grades: the product of a grade-$r$ and a
grade-$s$ element has grades $|r-s|, |r-s|+2, dots, r+s$, so a single product
of two multivectors generally touches several grades at once.

```ocaml
open Geom
let pi = 4. *. Float.atan 1. in
let sc x = Nx.scalar Nx.float32 x in
let v3 x y z = Nx.create Nx.float32 [| 3 |] [| x; y; z |] in

let u = E3.vector3 1. 0. 0.
and v = E3.vector3 (1. /. Float.sqrt 2.) (1. /. Float.sqrt 2.) 0. in
E3.to_array (E3.gp u v)   (* [|0.7071; 0.; 0.; 0.7071; 0.; 0.; 0.; 0.|] *)
```

Two facts make this the workhorse. First, associativity: $(a b) c = a (b c)$,
so products of many elements are unambiguous and can be evaluated left to
right. Second, invertibility is common: an element that is a product of
non-null vectors has an [[fa842ee6|inverse]], which is what makes
[[59a585e7|versors]] and hence motions possible.

Everything else is a projection. The [[8e33745c|The outer product spans subspaces]] is the top-grade part,
the [[f7e286b8|The left contraction is the metric projection]] the bottom-grade part, the [[79ec6893|The scalar product reads off a single number]] grade zero.
The [[25b7338e|The commutator makes bivectors a Lie algebra]] is the antisymmetric part, which is the Lie bracket that
generates motions.
