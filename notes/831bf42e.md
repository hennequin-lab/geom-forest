For orthogonal basis vectors with $i != j$, the [[3122fc58|Clifford relation]] gives

```typst
e_i e_j = -e_j e_i.
```

The product of two perpendicular directions is a bivector, and swapping the
order flips its sign. That sign is geometry: it is the orientation of the
plane.

```ocaml
open Geom

let e12 = E3.gp E3.e1 E3.e2 in
let e21 = E3.gp E3.e2 E3.e1 in
Nx.item [] (E3.scalar_prod e12 e21)   (* -1. *)
```

So a [[ad136f1c|bivector]] is an *ordered* pair of directions. $e_1 e_2$ and
$e_2 e_1$ are the same plane with opposite orientations, and a rotation by
$+theta$ in that plane is a rotation by $-theta$ in the other. This is why
[[b1f5db79|rotors]] have a sign ambiguity that no normalisation can remove
([[458a227d|Rotors compose by multiplication, twice around]]): a full turn of the sandwich brings you back to $-R$,
where $R$ is the rotor.

Non-commutativity is a feature. The [[25b7338e|The commutator makes bivectors a Lie algebra]] $A B - B A$ measures the
failure to commute, and for bivectors it is the Lie bracket of infinitesimal
motions. Rotations in different planes do not commute, and the commutator tells
you exactly how they fail — which is the algebra behind
[[6132d759|the SE(3) exponential]] and behind every kinematic Jacobian.

What does commute is scaled copies of a single blade: a bivector $B$ commutes
with itself, so the exponential $exp(B)$ is just a function of one bivector
([[0aa35a84|Exponentiating a simple bivector closes in three cases]]).
