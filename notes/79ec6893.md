Write $angle A angle_0$ for the grade-zero (scalar) part of a multivector $A$,
and $tilde(A)$ for its [[e00d2854|reverse]]. The scalar product of two
multivectors $A$ and $B$ is the single number

```typst
"scalar_prod"(A, B) = angle tilde(A) B angle_0.
```

It is symmetric in every signature, because reversing the product gives
$angle tilde(A) B angle_0 = angle tilde(B) A angle_0$. For vectors it coincides
with the [[f7e286b8|The left contraction is the metric projection]]: $angle tilde(u) v angle_0 = u dot v$. The library
exposes it as a function returning a tensor of the batch shape, because that is
usually what you want to compare against zero.

```ocaml
open Geom

let u = E3.vector3 1. 0. 0.
and v = E3.vector3 (1. /. Float.sqrt 2.) (1. /. Float.sqrt 2.) 0. in
Nx.item [] (E3.scalar_prod u v)   (* 0.7071 *)
```

The reason a library needs both a contraction and a scalar product is that they
differ on mixed grades and on blades in non-Euclidean signatures. The
contraction returns a multivector; the scalar product throws away everything
but the scalar. Incidence tests use the contraction because the *sign* of the
result is a signed distance ([[b24b87b3|Distance is an inner product]]), while normalisation uses
$angle tilde(A) A angle_0$, which is the squared magnitude of a blade up to the
sign convention of the reverse ([[e00d2854|Reverse and the magnitude of a blade]], [[fa842ee6|Invert a blade with its reverse]]). The library's
`norm2` is exactly that expression.

In the conformal model the scalar product of two points is the single most
useful number in the model: $-1 slash 2$ times their squared distance
([[b24b87b3|Distance is an inner product]]). That is why the library's `norm2` and `scalar_prod`
are the functions you reach for when you need a distance and do not want to
write one.
