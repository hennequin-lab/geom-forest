The regressive product (or meet) of multivectors $A$ and $B$ is the outer
product conjugated by the [[b5dd2ed2|In a degenerate algebra the dual becomes the complement]]:

```typst
A or B = (A^c and B^c)^c,
```

where $A^c$ is the signed index complement of $A$. It lowers grade instead of
raising it. Where the wedge spans the smallest subspace containing both
operands, the regressive product intersects them: two planes give their line, a
line and a plane give their point. This is the operation that makes projective
geometry a calculus rather than a collection of formulas.

```ocaml
open Geom

let pi1 = Pga.plane3 1. 0. 0. 0.    (* x = 0 *)
and pi2 = Pga.plane3 0. 1. 0. 0. in (* y = 0 *)
let l = Pga.meet pi1 pi2 in
Nx.to_array (Pga.line_direction l)          (* parallel to z *)
let joined = Pga.join (Pga.point3 0. 0. 2.) (Pga.point3 1. 0. 2.) in
Nx.to_array (Pga.line_direction joined)     (* (1,0,0) up to scale *)
```

In `Cl(3,0,1)` the regressive product is implemented exactly
(`Algebra.regressive`, exposed as `Pga.join`) and it is the *join* of points,
while the wedge is the *meet* of planes ([[1d71e490|Meet and join in PGA]]). The naming flips
because the model stores planes as vectors and points as trivectors. In
`Cl(4,1)` the star exists and the regressive product is available too, but the
conformal object layer prefers named constructors
([[5d4f04c6|Wedge the points, then dualise]]), because "wedge-then-dualise" is easier to audit than
a universal meet that silently accepts the wrong grades.
