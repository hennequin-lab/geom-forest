@@@ slug: motor
@@@ title: A motor is a dual quaternion
@@@ tags: ga/motion
@@@ aliases: motor, rigid motion, dual quaternion

A rigid motion is a rotation followed by a translation, $M = T R$. Here $R$ is
a rotor (a rotation about an axis through the origin) and $T$ is a translator
(a translation), both of them [[versor|versors]] of the conformal model. Their
product $M$ is a motor: an even unit versor of the subalgebra generated by
$e_1, e_2, e_3, e_oo$, where $e_1, e_2, e_3$ are the Euclidean basis vectors
and $e_oo$ is the point at infinity, the null vector $e_4 + e_5$ of `Cl(4,1)`.

The even part of that subalgebra is eight-dimensional, with basis

```typst
{1, e_12, e_13, e_23, e_1 e_oo, e_2 e_oo, e_3 e_oo, e_123 e_oo},
```

and it is isomorphic to the dual quaternions. This is the most important
implementation fact in the forest: a pose is eight numbers, not 32. The dense
even subalgebra of `Cl(4,1)` is sixteen-dimensional, and the rigid part is half
of it.

```ocaml
open Geom
let v3 x y z = Nx.create Nx.float32 [| 3 |] [| x; y; z |] in
let sc x = Nx.scalar Nx.float32 x in

let m =
  Motor.mul (Motor.translate3 0. 0. 1.)
    (Motor.rotate ~axis:(v3 0. 0. 1.) ~angle:(sc (Float.pi /. 2.)))
in
Nx.to_array (Motor.translation m)            (* [|0.; 0.; 1.|] *)
Nx.to_array (Cga.coords (Cga.apply_motor m (Cga.point3 1. 0. 0.)))
(* [|0.; 1.; 1.|] *)
```

`Motor.t` is a plain tensor of shape `batch @ [|8|]`, so it batches, traces and
differentiates like everything else ([[batching]]). It acts by the
[[sandwich]] $X |-> M X tilde(M)$, where $X$ is any object of the algebra and
$tilde(M)$ is the reverse of $M$; the action never materialises the
32-coefficient embedding, and it preserves grades and incidence.

Because the type is shared between the projective and conformal models
([[motor-shared]]), kinematics never has to choose a model. The mathematics is
in [[translator]] for the translation part, [[rotate-about-point]] for the
rotation part, and [[se3-exp]] for the general screw.

@@@ slug: motor-shared
@@@ title: One motor algebra, two models
@@@ tags: ga/motion
@@@ aliases: shared motor algebra

The subalgebra of `Cl(4,1)` generated by $e_1, e_2, e_3, e_oo$ is isomorphic to
`Cl(3,0,1)`, because the four generators have squares $1, 1, 1, 0$ and
anticommute. Here $e_oo = e_4 + e_5$ is the point at infinity and its partner in
`Cl(3,0,1)` is the degenerate basis vector $e_0$. Its even part is the eight-dimensional
[[motor|motor algebra]], with $e_oo$ playing the role of the degenerate $e_0$.

So the product, inverse, exponential, logarithm and slerp of motors are the
same code in both models. What is *not* shared is the object layer: a CGA point
is a null vector, a PGA point is a trivector, and the embeddings of the motor
into 32 or 16 coefficients differ by the sign convention of the dual part. The
library documents this as the $epsilon -> -epsilon$ automorphism of the dual
quaternions — the automorphism that flips the sign of the dual part — so the
packed basis elements $e_i e_0$ and $e_123 e_0$ are embedded with the opposite
sign in `Pga.of_motor`. The point is to align conventions: a translator by
$bold(t)$ must move a PGA point in the $+bold(t)$ direction.

```ocaml
open Geom

let m = Motor.translate3 1. 0. 0. in
Nx.to_array (Pga.point_coords (Pga.apply_motor m (Pga.point3 0. 0. 0.)))
(* [|1.; 0.; 0.|] *)
Nx.to_array (Cga.coords (Cga.apply_motor m (Cga.point3 0. 0. 0.)))
(* [|1.; 0.; 0.|] *)
```

The library therefore puts `Motor` at the top level and gives each model its own
`of_motor` and `apply_motor`. Reach for the dense version only when you need to
compose a motor with something that is not a motor — a
[[dilator|dilation]], a [[transversion]], or an arbitrary
[[versor|versor]] — or when you want the dense [[adjoint]].

This split is also the answer to "which model should I use?" for pure
kinematics: neither. Use `Motor` ([[cga-tradeoffs]]).

@@@ slug: translator
@@@ title: A translator is a null rotation
@@@ tags: ga/motion
@@@ aliases: translation, translator

A translation by the vector $bold(t) = t_1 e_1 + t_2 e_2 + t_3 e_3$ is
generated by the bivector $-1 slash 2 bold(t) e_oo$, where $e_oo = e_4 + e_5$
is the point at infinity (a null vector). That bivector is null,
because

```typst
(bold(t) e_oo)^2 = norm(bold(t))^2 e_oo^2 = 0,
```

so the exponential terminates after one term:

```typst
T = exp(-1/2 bold(t) e_oo) = 1 - 1/2 bold(t) e_oo.
```

This is the third case of [[bivector-exp|the bivector exponential]], and it is
why translation is cheap in this formalism: a translation is a *null rotation*,
an even versor with a nilpotent generator, not an affine trick appended from
outside.

```ocaml
open Geom

let t = Motor.translate3 0. 0. 1. in
Motor.to_array t                (* [|1.; 0.; 0.; 0.; 0.; 0.; -0.5; 0.|] *)
Nx.to_array (Cga.coords (Cga.apply_motor t (Cga.point3 0. 0. 0.)))
(* [|0.; 0.; 1.|] *)
```

A translator fixes $e_oo$ as a vector, which is exactly the statement that
translations are Euclidean motions ([[point-at-infinity]]). Its sandwich moves
a point by $bold(t)$ and adjusts the $e_oo$ coefficient by exactly the
$1 slash 2 norm(bold(t))^2$ needed to keep the image null — the correction that
an affine matrix does with a homogeneous coordinate and that the conformal
embedding does for free.

Translations compose by addition of their vectors, and they do not commute with
rotations, which is why a general pose needs both parts
([[motor-composition]], [[se3-exp]]).

@@@ slug: rotate-about-point
@@@ title: Rotating about an axis through a point
@@@ tags: ga/motion
@@@ aliases: rotation about a point, conjugation by a translator

A rotor $R$ about the origin, and a translator $T_C$ by the position vector
$bold(c)$ of a point $C$, combine into a rotation about the axis through $C$:

```typst
M = T_C R T_C^(-1).
```

That conjugation is the general pattern for turning an origin-centred motion
into a motion about another frame, and it is the same formula as a change of
basis in linear algebra. The result is a motor with both rotational and
translational parts.

```ocaml
open Geom
let v3 x y z = Nx.create Nx.float32 [| 3 |] [| x; y; z |] in
let sc x = Nx.scalar Nx.float32 x in

let about_point ~c ~axis ~angle =
  let t = Motor.translate c in
  let r = Motor.rotate ~axis ~angle in
  Motor.mul t (Motor.mul r (Motor.inverse t))

let m = about_point ~c:(v3 1. 0. 0.) ~axis:(v3 0. 0. 1.)
                  ~angle:(sc (Float.pi /. 2.)) in
Nx.to_array (Cga.coords (Cga.apply_motor m (Cga.point3 2. 0. 0.)))
(* [|1.; 1.; 0.|]: the point swings round the centre at (1,0,0) *)
```

In a kinematic chain the same idea appears as the *fixed* transform between link
frames, and each joint is either a rotor about its own local axis or a
translator along it ([[fk]]). A screw joint is the general case: the
exponential of a twist that has both parts ([[se3-exp]]).

Note that the order of the product is the matrix order ([[motor-composition]]),
and that conjugation preserves the versor structure, so the result is a motor of
the same norm; no renormalisation is needed for a single composition, though
long products should call `Motor.normalize` ([[numerics]]).

@@@ slug: motor-composition
@@@ title: Motors compose like matrices
@@@ tags: ga/motion
@@@ aliases: motor composition

`Motor.mul m1 m2` is the motion that applies `m2` first and `m1` second, so
the order matches matrices acting on column vectors:

```typst
"apply" (m_1 m_2) x = "apply" m_1 ("apply" m_2 x).
```

Here $m_1, m_2$ are motors and $x$ is any object of the algebra. The identity
element is `Motor.one`, and the inverse is `Motor.inverse`.

```ocaml
open Geom
let v3 x y z = Nx.create Nx.float32 [| 3 |] [| x; y; z |] in
let sc x = Nx.scalar Nx.float32 x in

let tz = Motor.translate3 0. 0. 1. in
let rz = Motor.rotate ~axis:(v3 0. 0. 1.) ~angle:(sc (Float.pi /. 2.)) in
let m = Motor.mul tz rz in
let same = Motor.compose [ tz; rz ] in
Nx.to_array (Cga.coords (Cga.apply_motor same (Cga.point3 1. 0. 0.)))
(* [|0.; 1.; 1.|]: rotate first, then lift *)
```

`Motor.compose` is `mul` folded over a list, rightmost applied first, and it
raises on the empty list rather than inventing an identity. `Motor.inverse`
gives the inverse motion; for a unit motor it is the reverse.

Three habits keep composition code correct. Keep the reading order explicit in
variable names (`shoulder`, `elbow`) rather than relying on comments. Remember
that a chain's product accumulates in link-local frames, so the fixed offsets
belong *between* the joint motors ([[fk]]). And renormalise after a long
product: float32 drift is small but real, and a motor that is no longer unit
makes `Motor.translation` slightly wrong ([[numerics]]).

@@@ slug: se3-exp
@@@ title: The SE(3) exponential turns a twist into a screw
@@@ tags: ga/motion
@@@ aliases: SE(3) exponential, twist, screw motion

A general element of the rigid Lie algebra is a twist
$xi = (omega, bold(v)) in RR^6$, with angular part $omega in RR^3$ and linear
part $bold(v) in RR^3$. Its exponential is a screw motion. In matrix form, let
$theta = norm(omega)$ be the rotation angle and let
$Omega = hat(omega)$ be the skew-symmetric matrix of $omega$, so that
$Omega bold(u) = omega times bold(u)$ for any vector $bold(u)$. Then

```typst
V = "Id" + (1 - cos theta)/theta^2 Omega
        + (theta - sin theta)/theta^3 Omega^2,
```

and the motion is $R = exp(Omega)$, $bold(t) = V bold(v)$ and
$M = (R, bold(t))$. The matrix $V$ is the left Jacobian of the exponential: it
turns a constant body velocity into the net translation. The implementation
uses this closed form with small-angle limits at zero ([[numerics]]).

```ocaml
open Geom

let twist = Nx.create Nx.float32 [| 6 |]
              [| 0.; 0.; Float.pi /. 2.; 0.; 0.; 1. |] in
let m = Motor.exp twist in
Nx.to_array (Motor.log m)      (* [|0.; 0.; 1.571; 0.; 0.; 1.|] *)
Nx.to_array (Cga.coords (Cga.apply_motor m (Cga.point3 1. 0. 0.)))
(* [|0.; 1.; 1.|]: a quarter turn about z, then a unit lift *)
```

The exponential is the bridge between the two languages robotics uses. In the
Lie-algebra language, a twist is a velocity and the exponential integrates it
over one unit of time. In the algebra language, the twist is a bivector and the
exponential is [[bivector-exp|the bivector exponential]]. The library exposes
the dense version too, and the tests check that the two agree — but the packed
`Motor.exp` is what kinematics should call, because it works in eight
coefficients rather than 32.

The screw interpretation is worth keeping: any rigid motion is a rotation about
some axis in space, combined with a translation along that same axis. The
exponential makes that decomposition algorithmic, and the logarithm
([[se3-log]]) recovers it.

@@@ slug: se3-log
@@@ title: The logarithm recovers the twist
@@@ tags: ga/motion
@@@ aliases: SE(3) logarithm

The logarithm is the inverse of [[se3-exp|the exponential]] near the identity.
Given a rotation matrix $R$ and a translation $bold(t)$, with
$theta = arccos((op("tr") R - 1)/2)$ the rotation angle, the twist is

```typst
omega = theta/(2 sin theta) "vee"(R - R^T), quad
bold(v) = V^(-1) bold(t).
```

Here $"vee"$ is the inverse of the hat map: it reads the three independent
entries of a skew-symmetric matrix back as a vector. The matrix $V$ is the left
Jacobian of [[se3-exp]], evaluated at $omega$:

```typst
V = "Id" + (1 - cos theta)/theta^2 Omega
        + (theta - sin theta)/theta^3 Omega^2,
```

with $Omega = hat(omega)$ the skew matrix of $omega$. A rotation by exactly
$pi$ is singular in the naive formula (both $sin theta$ and the skew part
vanish), so the implementation switches to a stable branch there.

```ocaml
open Geom

let m =
  Motor.mul (Motor.translate3 0. 0. 1.)
    (Motor.rotate ~axis:(Nx.create Nx.float32 [|3|] [|0.;0.;1.|])
       ~angle:(Nx.scalar Nx.float32 (Float.pi /. 2.)))
in
Nx.to_array (Motor.log m)   (* the twist, shape [|6|] *)
```

The logarithm is where most robotics code touches Lie theory: a pose error, a
velocity command, and an interpolation are all logarithms or exponentials. In
this forest it is the workhorse behind [[slerp|screw interpolation]] and
[[ik|inverse kinematics]].

Two cautions. The logarithm is multivalued globally — a rotation by $theta$ and
by $theta + 2 pi$ are the same motor but different twists — so "the" logarithm
always means the branch near the identity. And for a motor with no rotation
part the formula degenerates to the pure translation $bold(v) = bold(t)$, which
the small-angle branch handles. For the dense algebra the same map is
`Versor.log`, currently limited to a single simple bivector ([[bivector-exp]]).

@@@ slug: slerp
@@@ title: ScLERP interpolates along the group exponential
@@@ tags: ga/motion
@@@ aliases: ScLERP, screw interpolation

Because motors form a Lie group, interpolation is the group exponential:

```typst
M(lambda) = M_0 exp(lambda log(M_0^(-1) M_1)), quad lambda in [0, 1],
```

where $M_0$ is the start pose, $M_1$ the end pose, and $lambda$ the
interpolation parameter. This is screw linear interpolation: the motion follows
the screw that takes $M_0$ to $M_1$, at constant twist. The endpoints are exact
up to rounding. It is the motor analogue of quaternion slerp, and the reason
`exp` and `log` are in the public interface rather than buried in the
kinematics layer.

```ocaml
open Geom
let v3 x y z = Nx.create Nx.float32 [| 3 |] [| x; y; z |] in
let sc x = Nx.scalar Nx.float32 x in

let m0 = Motor.one in
let m1 =
  Motor.mul (Motor.translate3 1.3 0. 0.55)
    (Motor.rotate ~axis:(v3 0. 0. 1.) ~angle:(sc (Float.pi *. 0.85)))
in
let at t =
  Cga.coords (Cga.apply_motor (Motor.slerp m0 m1 (sc t)) (Cga.point3 0. 0. 0.))
in
Nx.to_array (at 0.5)   (* [|0.65; -0.5124; 0.275|] *)
```

![Five frames along a ScLERP: each is a rigid motion, and the screw axis is the
line the whole family shares.](../../figures/slerp-screw.svg)

Compared with interpolating the eight coefficients independently, ScLERP has
two properties that matter: the intermediate values are exact rigid motions,
and the path is independent of the frame the two endpoints were expressed in.
Compared with a straight line in translation plus a slerp in rotation, it is
the same thing only when the screw axis passes through the origin.

The parameter is a tensor, so a batch of interpolants is one call
([[batching]]). The endpoints $M_0$ and $M_1$ must be normalised motors, or the
logarithm absorbs the scale instead of the motion ([[numerics]]).

@@@ slug: adjoint
@@@ title: The adjoint turns a sandwich into a matrix
@@@ tags: ga/motion
@@@ aliases: adjoint

For a fixed versor $V$, the sandwich $X |-> V X V^(-1)$ is linear in $X$, so it
is a matrix in the blade basis. That matrix is the adjoint. For the dense
algebra it is $32 times 32$; for a motor it is the usual $6 times 6$ adjoint of
`SE(3)` acting on twists.

```ocaml
open Geom
let v3 x y z = Nx.create Nx.float32 [| 3 |] [| x; y; z |] in
let sc x = Nx.scalar Nx.float32 x in

let m = Motor.rotate ~axis:(v3 0. 0. 1.) ~angle:(sc (Float.pi /. 2.)) in
Nx.shape (Motor.adjoint m)          (* [|6; 6|] *)
Nx.shape (Cga.adjoint (Cga.of_motor m))  (* [|32; 32|] *)
```

Two uses. The first is composition of many motions applied to many points: a
$32 times 32$ matrix times a batch of 32-vectors is one `dot`, which is often
faster than a batched sandwich. The second is the Lie-theoretic one: the
adjoint carries twists (and, dually, wrenches) between frames, and the adjoint
of a composition is the product of the adjoints,
$op("Ad")(M_1 M_2) = op("Ad")(M_1) op("Ad")(M_2)$.

The dense adjoint is also the cleanest test of the sandwich implementation:
apply the sandwich to each basis blade, stack the results, and compare with the
matrix. That is a cheap property test, and it catches sign errors that a
hand-written example would miss ([[numerics]]).

@@@ slug: fk
@@@ title: Forward kinematics is a motor product
@@@ tags: ga/motion
@@@ aliases: forward kinematics

A kinematic chain is a product of motors. For each joint $i$, let $q_i$ be its
configuration, let $M_i (q_i)$ be that joint's motor — a rotor about an axis
through a point for a revolute joint, a translator for a prismatic one, the
exponential of a twist for a helical one — let $F_i$ be the fixed transform
from the previous link frame to the joint, and let $F_(n+1)$ be the fixed
transform from the last joint to the tool. Then

```typst
M(q) = F_1 M_1 (q_1) F_2 M_2 (q_2) dots F_n M_n (q_n) F_(n + 1),
```

where $n$ is the number of joints and $M(q)$ is the pose of the tool frame in
the base frame, and $q = (q_1, dots, q_n)$ is the vector of joint
configurations. The pose of any point or object attached to the tool is one
[[sandwich]].

```ocaml
open Geom
let v3 x y z = Nx.create Nx.float32 [| 3 |] [| x; y; z |] in
let sc x = Nx.scalar Nx.float32 x in

let lengths = [| 0.65; 0.55; 0.45; 0.35; 0.30; 0.25 |] in
let q = [| 0.55; -0.75; 0.95; 0.35; -0.85; 0.65 |] in
let axis i = if i land 1 = 0 then v3 0. 0. 1. else v3 0. 1. 0. in
let joint i =
  Motor.mul (Motor.rotate ~axis:(axis i) ~angle:(sc q.(i)))
            (Motor.translate3 0. 0. lengths.(i))
in
let rec arm i =
  if i = Array.length lengths then Motor.one
  else Motor.mul (joint i) (arm (i + 1))
in
Nx.to_array (Cga.coords (Cga.apply_motor (arm 0) (Cga.point3 0. 0. 0.)))
(* [-0.9564, -0.2967, 2.156] *)
```

![A planar arm built from motor products; the end-effector frame is one
sandwich away from the pose motor.](../../figures/fk-chain.svg)

The order of the product matters and is easy to get wrong
([[motor-composition]]). Every operation here is a product of eight-component
motors followed by a 32-component sandwich, both of which are small, batched and
differentiable ([[batching]], [[traced]]). The chain is a straight-line program
in the joint coordinates, so `Rune.grad` gives its task-space Jacobian without
hand-written derivatives, which is exactly what [[ik|inverse kinematics]]
exploits.

@@@ slug: ik
@@@ title: Inverse kinematics is a log-and-step loop
@@@ tags: ga/motion
@@@ aliases: inverse kinematics

A local inverse-kinematics step is a Gauss–Newton iteration on a pose error. In
motors, the error is a twist computed by the logarithm
([[se3-log]]), and the update is applied by the exponential
([[se3-exp]]):

```typst
xi = log(M_"target" M_"current"^(-1)), quad
q <- q + J^+ xi.
```

Here $M_"target"$ is the desired pose, $M_"current"$ the current pose, $xi$ the
six-vector pose error, $q$ the vector of joint coordinates, $J^+$ the
pseudo-inverse of the task Jacobian, and $J^+ xi$ the joint update. The error
$log(M_"target" M_"current"^(-1))$ is expressed in the world frame, because the
left factor $M_"target" M_"current"^(-1)$ acts on the left. For a tool-frame
error, reverse the order: $log(M_"current"^(-1) M_"target")$.

The Jacobian columns are the joint twists expressed in the same frame as the
error, either analytic or obtained by differentiating the chain ([[fk]]) with
`Rune`.

```ocaml
open Geom

(* the pose error as a twist, in the world frame *)
let error_twist ~target ~current =
  Motor.log (Motor.mul target (Motor.inverse current))   (* shape [|6|] *)

(* move a joint twist into the tool frame *)
let to_tool_frame m xi =
  Motor.log (Motor.mul (Motor.mul m (Motor.exp xi)) (Motor.inverse m))
```

The essential structure is four calls: `Motor.inverse` for the current pose,
`Motor.log` for the error, `Motor.adjoint` to express the error in the right
frame ([[adjoint]]), and `Motor.exp` for the update. Everything else is linear
algebra, and it is ordinary `Nx`, so the same loop runs on CPU or GPU and
batches over several targets at once ([[batching]]).

The two classic failure modes survive: local minima (the log branch is only
valid near the identity) and joint limits (a constraint on $q$, not on the
algebra). Neither is a defect of the representation; both are why the step is
usually damped.

@@@ slug: ga-residuals
@@@ title: Write task objectives as conformal residuals
@@@ tags: ga/motion
@@@ aliases: conformal residuals, constraints

The statement that a point $X$ lies on a sphere $S$, a plane $pi$, a line $l$
or a circle $z$ is the single scalar equation $X dot O = 0$, where $O$ is the
object blade in the [[working-representation|working representation]]. A distance is
$-2 X dot Y$ under a square root, with $Y$ the second point. A point-to-plane
signed distance is $X dot pi$. Each of these is a
[[polynomial-predicates|polynomial]] in the coefficients, so each is a residual
you can stack, weight, differentiate and solve.

```ocaml
open Geom
let v3 x y z = Nx.create Nx.float32 [| 3 |] [| x; y; z |] in
let sc x = Nx.scalar Nx.float32 x in

let sphere = Cga.sphere ~center:(v3 0. 0. 0.) ~radius:(sc 1.) in
let plane  = Cga.plane ~normal:(v3 0. 0. 1.) ~offset:(sc 0.5) in
let objective p =
  let x = Cga.point p in
  let a = Cga.scalar_prod x sphere in    (* zero on the sphere *)
  let b = Cga.scalar_prod x plane in     (* signed distance to the plane *)
  Nx.add (Nx.mul a a) (Nx.mul b b)

let value, grad = Rune.value_and_grad' objective (v3 0. 0. 0.)
```

The design constraint that makes this work is the
[[working-representation|working representation]]: one incidence expression for
every object type. The residuals differ only in which blade you contract
against, so a task-space objective is a list of contractions and a task-space
Jacobian is their gradient.

Two things to watch. The residuals are homogeneous in each object's
representative, so normalise the objects first (`Cga.normalize_sphere`,
`Cga.normalize_plane`) or the weighting will drift with the scale
([[numerics]]). And a residual that is zero can also be satisfied by the
degenerate object, so constraints on a point pair or a circle should be
accompanied by a non-degeneracy check ([[point-pair]], [[cga-circle]]).
