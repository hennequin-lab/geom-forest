@@@ slug: why-ga
@@@ title: Why geometric algebra: one product instead of a pile
@@@ tags: ga/foundation
@@@ aliases: motivation

Classical engineering geometry is a pile of unrelated operations. The dot
product measures alignment. The cross product measures perpendicularity but
only in three dimensions, and it hands back a normal vector where the honest
answer is a plane. Quaternions rotate. Homogeneous matrices translate. Plücker
coordinates describe lines, in a formalism with a constraint nobody remembers.
Each tool has its own composition rule, its own interpolation, its own
singularities, and its own trick for differentiating through it.

Geometric algebra replaces the pile with one associative product on one vector
space. Given two vectors, [[geometric-product|the geometric product]] $u v$
splits into a symmetric part, the metric, and an antisymmetric part, the
[[outer-product|wedge]] $u and v$. Every classical operation is a
[[grades|grade selection]] of that product or a
[[sandwich|sandwich]] by a product of vectors. A plane is a first-class object
(the bivector) rather than the shadow of a normal, so the same formula works in
two, three and five dimensions.

```ocaml
open Geom

let u = E3.vector3 1. 0. 0. in
let v = E3.vector3 0. 1. 0. in
E3.scalar_prod u v   (* 0. : they are perpendicular *)
E3.op u v            (* e12: the plane they span *)
E3.gp u v            (* e12: the product, which here has no scalar part *)
```

Two consequences matter for software. Predicates become
[[polynomial-predicates|polynomial]], hence differentiable and vectorisable.
And objects and motions live in the same algebra, so one
[[sandwich]] transforms points, spheres, planes and lines without a case
analysis; [[three-models]] is the menu of spaces where that pays off.

The honest counterweight is [[alternatives|the comparison with matrices]]: for
poses alone, dual quaternions are exactly this, and for pure linear algebra a
matrix is fine. The gain is in mixing geometry with kinematics, which is
[[cga-tradeoffs|precisely the robotics case]].

@@@ slug: clifford-relation
@@@ title: The Clifford relation is the whole definition
@@@ tags: ga/foundation
@@@ aliases: Clifford relation, defining relation

A Clifford algebra is generated by a real vector space $V$ with a symmetric
bilinear form $g$, subject to one relation:

```typst
u v + v u = 2 g(u, v), quad u, v in V
```

Read it as a specification of how the product fails to commute. The
antisymmetric part of $u v$ is new structure; the symmetric part is the metric
you started with. Choose an orthonormal basis $e_1, dots, e_n$ with
$e_i^2 = sigma_i in {+1, -1, 0}$ and the relation becomes two rules:

```typst
e_i^2 = sigma_i, quad e_i e_j = -e_j e_i quad (i != j)
```

Associativity does the rest. Because the basis vectors anticommute, any word in
them can be sorted, and words with a repeated letter collapse to a scalar. The
survivors are the [[basis-blades|blades]] $e_A$ over strictly increasing index
sets, of which there are $2^n$. That count *is* the dimension of the algebra,
and it also fixes the storage layout: one coefficient per blade
([[multivectors]]).

Everything else in this forest is a consequence. [[anticommutation]] is the
second rule. [[signature]] is the choice of the $sigma_i$, the one free
parameter of the construction. [[outer-product|The wedge]] records the
antisymmetric part as an object, and [[geometric-product|the product]] keeps
both parts at once.

```ocaml
open Geom

let e1 = E3.e1 and e2 = E3.e2 in
let sq = E3.to_array (E3.gp e1 e1) in            (* 1 on the scalar blade *)
let anti = Nx.add (E3.gp e1 e2) (E3.gp e2 e1) in (* the zero multivector *)
```

@@@ slug: signature
@@@ title: A signature counts positive, negative and null directions
@@@ tags: ga/foundation

The signature is the diagonal of the metric in an orthonormal basis: a list of
$+1$, $-1$ and $0$ entries, counted as $(p, q, r)$. It is the only datum the
algebra needs beyond the dimension, and it decides which geometry you get.

```typst
e_i e_j + e_j e_i = 2 g_(i j), quad g = "diag"(sigma_1, dots, sigma_n)
```

Three cases earn names in this forest:

- $(3,0,0)$, written `Cl(3,0,0)`: three Euclidean directions, the rotation
  algebra [[e3|E3]].
- $(3,0,1)$, written `Cl(3,0,1)`: three Euclidean directions and one null
  direction, plane-based projective geometry [[pga|PGA]].
- $(4,1)$, written `Cl(4,1)`: four positive and one negative direction, the
  conformal model [[cga|CGA]].

A null direction ($sigma_i = 0$) is not a degeneracy to be avoided: it is what
makes [[pga-no-dual|the projective model]] projective. It also breaks the Hodge
star, because the [[pseudoscalar]] is no longer invertible; that is the whole
reason [[complement]] exists.

```ocaml
open Geom

let s = Signature.conformal3 in
Signature.to_string s                       (* "(4,1,0)" *)
(Signature.dim s, Signature.size s)         (* (5, 32) *)
Signature.pseudoscalar_square s             (* -1 *)
Signature.pseudoscalar_square (Signature.projective 3)  (* 0 *)
```

`Signature.make` rejects any entry that is not $-1$, $0$ or $1$, so an invalid
metric cannot reach the algebra. The models in the library fix the signature
once, at module initialisation, which lets them precompute the whole
[[structure-constants|multiplication table]].

@@@ slug: basis-blades
@@@ title: Basis blades are bitmasks
@@@ tags: ga/foundation
@@@ aliases: bitmask blades

A blade is an ordered product $e_(i_1) e_(i_2) dots e_(i_k)$ of distinct basis
vectors. Number the basis vectors from $1$ to $n$ and encode a blade by the
integer whose bit $i - 1$ is set exactly when $e_i$ occurs in it. Then:

- the [[grades|grade]] is the population count of the integer;
- the product index is the bitwise exclusive or, because a repeated letter
  cancels: $e_A e_B prop e_(A xor B)$;
- the sign is the parity of the permutation that sorts the concatenation of
  $A$ and $B$;
- the metric factor is the product of $sigma_i$ over the intersection
  $A inter B$, and is zero when a null direction appears in both factors.

This is the whole multiplication algorithm. For $n = 5$ there are $32$ blades
and $1024$ ordered pairs, so the [[structure-constants|table]] is 4 KiB of
integers, computed once. `Blade` owns the index arithmetic and knows nothing
about the metric, which is why it can be shared by all the models.

```ocaml
open Geom

let b = Blade.of_int 3 in
Blade.to_string b                     (* "e12" *)
Blade.grade b                         (* 2 *)
Blade.mul_sign b b                    (* -1: e12 e12 = -1 *)
Blade.to_string (Blade.complement ~n:3 b)  (* "e3" *)
Blade.complement_sign ~n:3 b          (* 1: e12 and e3 = e123 *)
```

`Blade.all n` returns the blades in increasing bitmask order, which is exactly
the order of the coefficient array of a [[multivectors|multivector]]. The
bitmask encoding is also why the library's storage choice in
[[orthonormal-storage]] matters: the xor rule needs orthogonality.

@@@ slug: grades
@@@ title: A blade is a subspace with a magnitude
@@@ tags: ga/foundation
@@@ aliases: grade, blades

The grade of a blade is the number of basis vectors in the product, so it is
also the dimension of the subspace the blade represents. A grade-1 blade is a
vector: a line through the origin, with a length. A grade-2 blade is a
bivector: an oriented plane, with an area. A grade-3 blade is a volume. In
$n$ dimensions the grades run from $0$ (scalars) to $n$ (the
[[pseudoscalar]]).

This is the conceptual difference from the cross product. $u times v$ is
perpendicular to the plane of $u$ and $v$ and only exists in three dimensions.
$u and v$ *is* that plane, oriented from $u$ toward $v$, with magnitude equal to
the area of the parallelogram. It exists in every dimension, and the plane does
not need a normal to be named.

```ocaml
open Geom

let b = E3.op E3.e1 E3.e2 in
let mixed = E3.gp E3.e1 (E3.vector3 1. 1. 1.) in   (* 1 + e12 + e13 *)
let grade_of_e12 = Blade.grade (Blade.of_int 3) in (* 2 *)
let scalar_part = E3.grade 0 mixed in              (* the scalar part *)
let odd_part = E3.odd b in                     (* zero: bivectors are even *)
(E3.to_array scalar_part, E3.to_array odd_part)
```

A general element is a sum of blades of different grades, a
[[multivectors|multivector]], and products mix them: the
[[geometric-product]] of a grade-$r$ and a grade-$s$ element contains every
grade $|r-s|, |r-s|+2, dots, r+s$. The [[outer-product]] keeps the top grade,
the [[contraction]] keeps the bottom one, and the
[[even-subalgebra]] keeps the even ones.

@@@ slug: multivectors
@@@ title: A multivector is a coefficient vector over blades
@@@ tags: ga/foundation
@@@ aliases: multivector, coefficient vector

A multivector is an element of the algebra: a formal sum
$A = sum_A A_A e_A$ over the [[basis-blades|blades]]. In software it is just
the array of its $2^n$ coefficients, in increasing bitmask order. Nothing about
the algebra is hidden in the type; the operations read and write that array.

```ocaml
open Geom

let a = E3.gp E3.e1 E3.e2 in
E3.to_array a                 (* [|0.; 0.; 0.; 1.; 0.; 0.; 0.; 0.|] *)
Blade.to_string (Blade.of_int 3)  (* "e12": index 3 has the coefficient 1 *)
E3.coeff a (Blade.of_int 3)   (* 1. *)
```

`coeff` and `with_coeff` are the accessors that name a blade directly, and
they are the API you want in tests and in debugging. `grade k` keeps one grade
and zeros the rest; `even` and `odd` do it for a whole parity.

The coefficient vector is also the batching axis convention: a multivector has
shape `[|2^n|]`, a batch of $b$ of them has shape `[|b; 2^n|]`, and an image
of points has shape `[|h; w; 2^n|]`. The leading axes are ordinary broadcast
axes for `Nx`, so every algebra operation is one batched contraction over the
last axis. [[batching]] spells out the consequences; [[orthonormal-storage]]
explains why the coefficient basis is the orthonormal one even in the conformal
model.

@@@ slug: geometric-product
@@@ title: The geometric product
@@@ tags: ga/foundation

The geometric product is the Clifford product: associative, distributive,
bilinear, and non-commutative. For two vectors it splits cleanly,

```typst
u v = u dot v + u and v,
```

into a scalar and a bivector. The scalar part is how much the vectors agree.
The bivector part is how much they disagree, and it records the plane. For
general elements the product mixes grades: the product of a grade-$r$ and a
grade-$s$ element has grades $|r-s|, |r-s|+2, dots, r+s$, so a single product
of two multivectors generally touches several grades at once.

```ocaml
open Geom
let pi = 4. *. Float.atan 1. in
let sc x = Nx.scalar Nx.float32 x in
let v3 x y z = Nx.create Nx.float32 [| 3 |] [| x; y; z |] in

let u = E3.vector3 1. 0. 0.
and v = E3.vector3 (1. /. Float.sqrt 2.) (1. /. Float.sqrt 2.) 0. in
E3.to_array (E3.gp u v)   (* [|0.7071; 0.; 0.; 0.7071; 0.; 0.; 0.; 0.|] *)
```

Two facts make this the workhorse. First, associativity: $(a b) c = a (b c)$,
so products of many elements are unambiguous and can be evaluated left to
right. Second, invertibility is common: an element that is a product of
non-null vectors has an [[blade-inverse|inverse]], which is what makes
[[versor|versors]] and hence motions possible.

Everything else is a projection. The [[outer-product]] is the top-grade part,
the [[contraction]] the bottom-grade part, the [[scalar-product]] grade zero.
The [[commutator]] is the antisymmetric part, which is the Lie bracket that
generates motions.

@@@ slug: outer-product
@@@ title: The outer product spans subspaces
@@@ tags: ga/foundation
@@@ aliases: wedge product, wedge, exterior product

The outer product is the grade-raising part of the
[[geometric-product|geometric product]]:

```typst
A and B = sum_(r,s) (A_r B_s)_(r+s).
```

It is associative, bilinear, and antisymmetric on vectors: $u and v = -(v and u)$
and $u and u = 0$. So $u and v$ is not "the area", it is the oriented
parallelogram spanned by $u$ and $v$, with the area as its magnitude. And a
wedge of $k$ independent vectors is a grade-$k$ blade, the subspace they span.
Dependence is visible algebraically: if the vectors are linearly dependent
their wedge is zero.

```ocaml
open Geom

(* coplanar with e12: the wedge vanishes *)
E3.to_array (E3.op (E3.vector3 1. 1. 0.) (E3.op E3.e1 E3.e2))
(* [|0.; 0.; 0.; 0.; 0.; 0.; 0.; 0.|] *)

(* a vector out of the plane spans the whole space *)
E3.to_array (E3.op E3.e3 (E3.op E3.e1 E3.e2))  (* one 1 on e123 *)
```

The wedge is the natural constructor of geometry, because it needs no metric:
it works unchanged in degenerate metrics, where the
[[hodge-dual]] does not exist. In [[pga|projective geometry]] it *is* the
incidence relation ([[pga-incidence]]), and in the conformal model
[[objects-from-points|objects are built by wedging points]]. The complement of
the wedge is the [[regressive|regressive product]].

@@@ slug: contraction
@@@ title: The left contraction is the metric projection
@@@ tags: ga/foundation
@@@ aliases: inner product, left contraction

The left contraction is the grade-lowering part of the
[[geometric-product|geometric product]]:

```typst
A dot B = sum_(r,s) (A_r B_s)_(s-r), quad s >= r.
```

On vectors it is the ordinary dot product, so it carries the metric. On a
vector and a bivector it does something more useful than a number: $e_1 dot e_12 = e_2$.
Contracting with $e_1$ projects onto the plane and rotates by a quarter turn
inside it. That is the geometric content of "multiply by $i$" in complex
numbers, promoted to any dimension.

```ocaml
open Geom

E3.to_array (E3.inner E3.e1 (E3.op E3.e1 E3.e2))  (* e2 *)
E3.to_array (E3.inner E3.e2 (E3.op E3.e1 E3.e2))  (* -e1 *)
E3.to_array (E3.inner E3.e3 (E3.op E3.e1 E3.e2))  (* zero: orthogonal *)
```

The contraction is what makes incidence a scalar equation in the conformal
model. A point $X$ lies on a sphere $S$, a plane $pi$, a line $ell$ or a circle
$z$ exactly when $X dot O = 0$ ([[conformal-distance]],
[[round-flat]]). It is also the natural way to extract the geometric content of
an object blade: $e_oo dot z$ for a circle $z$ is its carrier plane
([[circle-carrier]]).

The library calls it `inner`, and `scalar_prod` is its grade-zero companion.

@@@ slug: scalar-product
@@@ title: The scalar product reads off a single number
@@@ tags: ga/foundation
@@@ aliases: scalar product

The scalar product is the grade-zero part of the product,
$angle tilde(A) B angle_0$, a single number per pair of multivectors. It is
symmetric in an Euclidean signature, and for vectors it coincides with the
[[contraction]]: $u dot v$. The library exposes it as a function returning a
tensor of the batch shape, because that is usually what you want to compare
against zero.

```ocaml
open Geom

let u = E3.vector3 1. 0. 0.
and v = E3.vector3 (1. /. Float.sqrt 2.) (1. /. Float.sqrt 2.) 0. in
Nx.item [] (E3.scalar_prod u v)   (* 0.7071 *)
```

The reason a library needs both a contraction and a scalar product is that they
differ on mixed grades and on blades in non-Euclidean signatures. The
contraction returns a multivector; the scalar product throws away everything
but the scalar. Incidence tests use the contraction because the *sign* of the
result is a signed distance ([[conformal-distance]]), while normalisation uses
$angle tilde(A) A angle_0$, which is the squared magnitude of a blade
([[reverse]], [[blade-inverse]]).

In the conformal model the scalar product of two points is the single most
useful number in the model: $-1/2$ times their squared distance
([[conformal-distance]]). The library's `norm2` is the same function with both
arguments equal.

@@@ slug: anticommutation
@@@ title: Perpendicular directions anticommute
@@@ tags: ga/foundation
@@@ aliases: anticommutation, non-commutativity

For orthogonal basis vectors with $i != j$ the [[clifford-relation|Clifford
relation]] gives $e_i e_j = -e_j e_i$. The product of two perpendicular
directions is a bivector, and swapping the order flips its sign. That sign is
geometry: it is the orientation of the plane.

```ocaml
open Geom

let e12 = E3.gp E3.e1 E3.e2 in
let e21 = E3.gp E3.e2 E3.e1 in
Nx.item [] (E3.scalar_prod e12 e21)   (* -1. *)
```

So a [[grades|bivector]] is an *ordered* pair of directions. $e_1 e_2$ and
$e_2 e_1$ are the same plane with opposite orientations, and a rotation by
$+theta$ in that plane is a rotation by $-theta$ in the other. This is why
[[rotor|rotors]] have a sign ambiguity that no normalisation can remove
([[rotor-composition]]): a full turn of the sandwich brings you back to $-R$.

Non-commutativity is a feature. The [[commutator]] $A B - B A$ measures the
failure to commute, and for bivectors it is the Lie bracket of infinitesimal
motions. Rotations in different planes do not commute, and the commutator tells
you exactly how they fail — which is the algebra behind
[[se3-exp|the SE(3) exponential]] and behind every kinematic Jacobian.

What does commute is scaled copies of a single blade: $B$ commutes with itself,
so the exponential $exp(B)$ is just a function of one bivector
([[bivector-exp]]).

@@@ slug: reverse
@@@ title: Reverse and the magnitude of a blade
@@@ tags: ga/foundation
@@@ aliases: reverse

Reverse turns every product around: $tilde(A B) = tilde(B) tilde(A)$. On a
grade-$k$ blade it is a sign,

```typst
tilde(e_A) = (-1)^(k (k - 1) / 2) e_A,
```

so scalars and vectors are unchanged, bivectors and trivectors flip sign, and
four-blades are unchanged again. The sign pattern $+ + - - + + dots$ is the one
that makes a blade's *magnitude* come out positive:

```typst
abs(A) = sqrt(abs(angle tilde(A) A angle_0)).
```

```ocaml
open Geom

E3.to_array (E3.reverse (E3.op E3.e1 E3.e2))   (* -e12 *)
Nx.item [] (E3.norm2 (E3.op E3.e1 E3.e2))      (* 1. *)
Nx.item [] (E3.norm (E3.op (E3.vector3 2. 0. 0.)
                            (E3.vector3 0. 3. 0.)))      (* 6. *)
```

For a blade, $tilde(A) A$ is always a scalar, which is why the
[[blade-inverse|inverse of a blade]] is just $tilde(A)$ divided by that
number. For a general versor, the reverse is the inverse up to a sign:
[[versor|unit versors]] satisfy $V tilde(V) = plus.minus 1$. That is what makes
[[sandwich|the sandwich]] cheap: the inverse is a reverse, no linear solve.

Reverse is one of [[involutions|three standard involutions]] — the only one that
is an anti-automorphism rather than an automorphism.

@@@ slug: involutions
@@@ title: Three involutions: reverse, grade flip, conjugation
@@@ tags: ga/foundation
@@@ aliases: involutions, grade involution, Clifford conjugation

Three sign patterns on grade-$k$ blades do all the bookkeeping:

```typst
tilde(e_A) = (-1)^(k(k-1)/2) e_A, quad
hat(e_A) = (-1)^k e_A, quad
overline(e_A) = (-1)^(k(k+1)/2) e_A.
```

They are [[reverse]], grade involution and Clifford conjugation. Each is an
involution, each is determined by the grade, and each has one job.

- $hat(A)$ (grade involution) separates even from odd, so it is the projector
  behind [[even-subalgebra|the even subalgebra]].
- $tilde(A)$ (reverse) is the anti-automorphism that defines the magnitude and
  the inverse ([[blade-inverse]]).
- $overline(A) = tilde(hat(A))$ is the Clifford conjugate, the sign pattern
  $+ - - + + - - dots$. On motors it is the group inverse; on a
  [[quaternions|quaternion]] it is the usual conjugate.

```ocaml
open Geom

let b = E3.op E3.e1 E3.e2 in
Nx.item [] (E3.scalar_prod b (E3.reverse b))          (* 1. *)
Nx.item [] (E3.scalar_prod b (E3.grade_involution b)) (* -1. *)
```

The three compose: conjugation is reverse after grade involution, and any two
of them give the third up to the parity of the grade. The library exposes all
three (`reverse`, `grade_involution`, `conjugate`) plus the
`Blade.*_sign` helpers that the precomputed tables are built from.

@@@ slug: blade-inverse
@@@ title: Invert a blade with its reverse
@@@ tags: ga/foundation

For a blade $A$, the product $tilde(A) A$ is a scalar, so inverting is a
division:

```typst
A^(-1) = tilde(A) / (tilde(A) A).
```

A blade with $tilde(A) A = 0$ is *null* and has no inverse at all; a blade with
$tilde(A) A != 0$ is invertible and is called non-null. Null blades are not
pathological in this forest — the [[pga-no-dual|degenerate direction]] $e_0$ is
null, [[conformal-embedding|conformal points]] are null vectors, and
[[translator|translators]] are generated by a null bivector — they simply
cannot be normalised metrically.

```ocaml
open Geom

let b = E3.op (E3.vector3 2. 0. 0.) (E3.vector3 0. 3. 0.) in
Nx.item [] (E3.norm2 b)                       (* 36. *)
E3.to_array (E3.inverse b)                    (* -e12 / 6 *)
E3.to_array (E3.sandwich b b)                 (* b *)
```

`inverse` implements that formula and does not detect singularity, so
[[numerics|check `norm2` first]] when an object may be null. Because inverting
is a reverse, the [[involutions|three involutions]] do most of the sign
bookkeeping in the library. `normalize` is the *metric* normalisation
$A / abs(A)$; the projective normalisations of
[[cga-sphere|spheres]], [[cga-plane|planes]] and
[[pga-ideal-points|points]] are different operations with different jobs, and
mixing them up is the most common source of silently wrong geometry.

@@@ slug: pseudoscalar
@@@ title: The pseudoscalar is the whole space as one blade
@@@ tags: ga/foundation
@@@ aliases: pseudoscalar, I

The pseudoscalar is the top-grade blade, $I = e_1 e_2 dots e_n$: the whole
vector space, oriented. It has one coefficient, and its square is a single
number that characterises the signature:

```typst
I^2 = (-1)^(n(n-1)/2) (-1)^q.
```

In `Cl(3,0,0)`, $I^2 = -1$: the pseudoscalar behaves like the imaginary unit,
which is why the even subalgebra is the quaternions ([[quaternions]]). In
`Cl(4,1)`, $n = 5$ and $q = 1$, so $I^2 = -1$ again, and $I^(-1) = -I$. In
`Cl(3,0,1)` the square is $0$, because a null direction appears in the product.

```ocaml
open Geom

E3.to_array E3.pseudoscalar                     (* e123 *)
Nx.item [] (E3.scalar_prod E3.pseudoscalar E3.pseudoscalar)  (* -1. *)
Signature.pseudoscalar_square (Signature.projective 3)        (* 0 *)
```

Multiplication by $I$ is the [[hodge-dual|Hodge dual]] in a non-degenerate
algebra: it sends a grade-$k$ blade to a grade-$(n-k)$ blade representing the
orthogonal complement. When $I$ is not invertible the same map is still defined
by index complement alone, with precomputed signs, and it is called the
[[complement]]. That is the difference between the conformal and projective
object layers, and the reason [[pga-no-dual|PGA cannot use the star]].

@@@ slug: hodge-dual
@@@ title: The Hodge dual trades a blade for its complement
@@@ tags: ga/foundation
@@@ aliases: dual, Hodge star

When the metric is non-degenerate the [[pseudoscalar]] is invertible, and the
right Hodge dual of $A$ is

```typst
A^* = A I^(-1),
```

a blade of grade $n - k$ when $A$ has grade $k$. Geometrically it is the
orthogonal complement: a plane becomes its normal line, a point becomes the
pencil of planes through it. The sign convention matters because the star is
only an involution up to sign. In `Cl(4,1)` we have $I^2 = -1$, hence
$I^(-1) = -I$ and

```typst
(A^*)^* = -A, quad "undual"(A) = A I, quad "undual"(A^*) = A.
```

```ocaml
open Geom

let p = Cga.point3 1. 2. 3. in
let star = Cga.dual p in
Blade.grade (Blade.of_int 15)                 (* 4: the dual of a point *)
Nx.to_array (Cga.coeff (Cga.undual star) (Blade.of_int 1))  (* 1. *)
```

`undual` must be an explicit function, not `dual` twice, precisely because of
that minus sign. And `dual` raises for a degenerate signature, where $I$ has no
inverse: use [[complement]] there, and never mix the two.

The dual is how the conformal object layer stores the *lower* of two
representatives. [[working-representation]] is the table; [[objects-from-points]]
is the recipe that produces those duals from wedges of points.

@@@ slug: complement
@@@ title: In a degenerate algebra the dual becomes the complement
@@@ tags: ga/foundation
@@@ aliases: Poincare complement, complement

The Hodge star needs an invertible [[pseudoscalar]], which a
[[signature|degenerate signature]] forbids. What survives is index
complement: for each blade $e_A$, the blade $e_(A^c)$ whose index set is the
complement of $A$, signed so that

```typst
e_A and e_(A^c) = I.
```

That signed permutation of coefficients is the *complement*. It is defined for
every signature, it is cheap (a gather with precomputed signs), and it is the
dual that projective geometry actually uses.

```ocaml
open Geom

let b = Blade.of_int 3 in                      (* e12 *)
Blade.to_string (Blade.complement ~n:3 b)      (* "e3" *)
Blade.complement_sign ~n:3 b                   (* 1: e12 and e3 = e123 *)
Blade.to_string (Blade.complement ~n:4 (Blade.of_basis 1))  (* "e234" *)
```

Two properties to remember. First, the complement is *not* the star: sign
conventions differ and there is no metric involved beyond the index sets.
Second, because it needs no inverse, it composes safely with the
[[regressive|regressive product]], which is defined through it. In `Cl(4,1)`
the library exposes both, and [[numerics|the numerical note]] warns about
mixing them. In `Cl(3,0,1)` only the complement exists
([[pga-no-dual]]).

@@@ slug: regressive
@@@ title: The regressive product is the dual of the wedge
@@@ tags: ga/foundation
@@@ aliases: regressive product, meet

The regressive product (or meet) is the outer product conjugated by the
[[complement]]:

```typst
A or B = (A^c and B^c)^c.
```

It lowers grade instead of raising it. Where the wedge spans the smallest
subspace containing both operands, the regressive product intersects them: two
planes give their line, a line and a plane give their point. This is the
operation that makes projective geometry a calculus rather than a collection of
formulas.

```ocaml
open Geom

let pi1 = Pga.plane3 1. 0. 0. 0. in
let pi2 = Pga.plane3 0. 1. 0. 0. in
let l = Pga.join (Pga.point3 0. 0. 2.) (Pga.point3 1. 0. 2.) in
Pga.line_direction l                        (* (1,0,0) up to scale *)
```

In `Cl(3,0,1)` the regressive product is implemented exactly
(`Algebra.regressive`, exposed as `Pga.join`) and it is the *join* of points,
while the wedge is the *meet* of planes ([[pga-meet-join]]). The naming flips
because the model stores planes as vectors and points as trivectors. In
`Cl(4,1)` the star exists and the regressive product is available too, but the
conformal object layer prefers named constructors
([[objects-from-points]]), because "wedge-then-dualise" is easier to audit than
a universal meet that silently accepts the wrong grades.

@@@ slug: versor
@@@ title: A versor is an invertible product of vectors
@@@ tags: ga/foundation
@@@ aliases: versor, spin group

A versor is a product of invertible vectors, $V = v_1 v_2 dots v_k$. Versors
are the elements that act on the algebra by conjugation, and they are exactly
the elements with an inverse of the form $plus.minus tilde(V)$. After
normalisation, $V tilde(V) = plus.minus 1$, so the
[[blade-inverse|inverse]] is a [[reverse|reverse]] and applying a versor costs
two products, not a linear solve.

The versors of a given signature form a group, and its identity component is
the spin group: $"Spin"(3,0,0) tilde.eq "SU"(2)$ double-covers rotations,
$"Spin"(4,1)$ double-covers the Möbius group of $RR^3$
([[mobius-group]]). Even versors are [[even-subalgebra|even]], and they are
called rotors when they are Euclidean [[rotor|rotations]], motors when they are
rigid motions ([[motor]]), translators when they are translations
([[translator]]).

```ocaml
open Geom

let r = E3.rotor ~axis:(Nx.create Nx.float32 [| 3 |] [| 0.; 0.; 1. |])
                 ~angle:(Nx.scalar Nx.float32 (Float.pi /. 3.)) in
Nx.item [] (E3.scalar_prod r (E3.reverse r))   (* 1.: unit *)
Nx.to_array (E3.to_vector (E3.sandwich r E3.e1))
```

The exponential of a bivector is the standard way to manufacture a versor
([[bivector-exp]]), and the logarithm inverts it ([[se3-log]]). `Versor.Make`
is the functor that provides `exp`, `log`, `exp_simple` and `slerp` for any
algebra.

@@@ slug: sandwich
@@@ title: The sandwich is an algebra automorphism
@@@ tags: ga/foundation
@@@ aliases: sandwich product, conjugation action

A versor $V$ acts on the algebra by

```typst
X |-> V X V^(-1),
```

the sandwich. Because it is conjugation by an invertible element, it is an
algebra automorphism: it preserves grades, products, sums and therefore every
incidence relation. It is also linear in $X$, which means it has a matrix
([[adjoint]]).

```ocaml
open Geom

let m = Motor.translate3 0. 0. 1. in
let s = Cga.sphere ~center:(Nx.create Nx.float32 [| 3 |] [| 0.; 0.; 0. |])
                   ~radius:(Nx.scalar Nx.float32 2.) in
let s' = Cga.apply_motor m s in
Nx.to_array (Cga.coords (Cga.sphere_center s'))   (* [|0.; 0.; 1.|] *)
```

This is the single action in the library. `apply_rotor`, `apply_motor` and
`Algebra.sandwich` are the same operation with different knowledge of the
inverse: a rotor's inverse is its reverse, a motor's is its conjugate, and a
general versor's is `tilde(V) / <tilde(V) V>_0`.

Because the action is uniform, there is no per-object transform function in the
library, and no risk of transforming a sphere correctly and its centre
incorrectly. That uniformity is the practical payoff of
[[why-ga|the whole enterprise]], and it is what
[[viz-adapters|the renderer]] exploits when it draws any object at all.

@@@ slug: even-subalgebra
@@@ title: The even subalgebra holds the rotations
@@@ tags: ga/foundation
@@@ aliases: even subalgebra

The even-grade elements of a Clifford algebra close under multiplication, so
they form a subalgebra of dimension $2^(n-1)$. It contains the scalars and the
bivectors, hence the [[bivector-exp|exponentials of bivectors]], hence all
[[rotor|rotors]]: every rotation is an even versor.

Two instances are worth memorising:

- the even subalgebra of `Cl(3,0,0)` is `Cl(0,2,0)`, the
  [[quaternions|quaternions]];
- the even subalgebra of `Cl(3,0,1)` is the eight-dimensional
  [[motor|rigid-motion algebra]], the dual quaternions.

```ocaml
open Geom

let g = E3.gp E3.e1 E3.e2 in
Blade.to_string (Blade.of_int 3)               (* "e12" *)
E3.to_array (E3.odd g)                         (* zero: even *)
let r = E3.rotor ~axis:(Nx.create Nx.float32 [|3|] [|0.;0.;1.|])
                 ~angle:(Nx.scalar Nx.float32 0.5) in
E3.to_array (E3.odd (E3.gp r E3.e1))           (* all of it: odd *)
```

The odd part is a module over the even part: multiplying an even element by an
odd one stays odd. That is why a rotor can rotate a vector (odd to odd) while
being even itself, and why reversing a sandwich does not change the parity of
the object.

The [[grades|grade]] split also suggests the first software optimisation: if
every value you need is even, you only need half the coefficients. The library
does that explicitly for motors, storing 8 components instead of 16 or 32
([[motor-shared]]).

@@@ slug: bivector-exp
@@@ title: Exponentiating a simple bivector closes in three cases
@@@ tags: ga/foundation
@@@ aliases: exponential of a bivector, exp

A bivector $B$ generates a one-parameter group of versors through
$exp(B) = sum_(k >= 0) B^k slash k!$. When $B$ is *simple* — $B and B = 0$,
equivalently $B^2$ is a scalar — the series terminates in a closed form:

```typst
exp(B) = cases(
  cos(theta) + sin(theta)/theta B & "if" B^2 = -theta^2 < 0,
  cosh(theta) + sinh(theta)/theta B & "if" B^2 = +theta^2 > 0,
  1 + B & "if" B^2 = 0,
)
```

The three cases are the three geometries a single plane can carry. A negative
square is an elliptic rotation: that is [[rotor|a rotor]]. A positive square is
a hyperbolic boost: a translation in the conformal model
([[translator]]) or a dilation ([[dilator]]). A zero square is a null
rotation: a translation again, or a transversion ([[transversion]]), or a shear.

```ocaml
open Geom

module V3 = Versor.Make (E3)

let b = E3.gp (E3.op E3.e1 E3.e2) (E3.scalar (-.Float.pi /. 4.)) in
let r = V3.exp_simple b in                 (* a 45 degree rotor *)
Nx.to_array (E3.to_vector (E3.apply_rotor r E3.e1))
```

A general bivector in five dimensions is a sum of at most two commuting simple
bivectors, so the same closed form applies after a spectral decomposition. The
library implements `exp_simple` today and stages the general
`exp`/`log` behind the `Versor` interface; the robotics path instead uses the
packed [[se3-exp|`Motor.exp`]], which is the same map on a smaller algebra
([[motor-shared]]).

@@@ slug: commutator
@@@ title: The commutator makes bivectors a Lie algebra
@@@ tags: ga/foundation
@@@ aliases: commutator product, Lie bracket

The commutator product is half the antisymmetric part of the
[[geometric-product|geometric product]]:

```typst
A times B = (A B - B A)/2.
```

On bivectors it is the Lie bracket of the Lie algebra $"so"(p,q)$, and it
measures how two infinitesimal rotations fail to commute. The
[[even-subalgebra]]'s bivectors are the Lie algebra of the spin group, so the
commutator is the algebraic source of every structure you would otherwise
import from Lie theory: generators of motion, the adjoint action and the
Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff expansion behind [[se3-exp|the exponential]].

```ocaml
open Geom

let b12 = E3.op E3.e1 E3.e2 in
let b23 = E3.op E3.e2 E3.e3 in
E3.to_array (E3.commutator b12 b23)   (* -e13: the third rotation plane *)
```

In the conformal model the six bivectors $e_(i j)$ and $e_i and e_oo$ close
under the commutator into the Lie algebra of `SE(3)`; adding $e_4 e_5$ and the
three $e_i and e_0$ gives the ten-dimensional
[[mobius-group|conformal algebra]]. That is the whole reason a
[[motor|motor]] can carry a Lie-group structure while looking like a
multivector.

The library exposes it as `Algebra.commutator`, with no claim beyond the
bivector case.

@@@ slug: polynomial-predicates
@@@ title: Geometric predicates are polynomial
@@@ tags: ga/foundation
@@@ aliases: polynomial predicates, differentiability

Every predicate in this forest is built from sums and products of the
coefficients. Incidence is a [[contraction]] against a blade, distance is a
[[scalar-product]], intersection is a
[[outer-product|wedge]], and a transformation is a [[sandwich]]. The only
non-polynomial operations are the final square roots and trigonometric
functions inside a constructor.

This matters because polynomial residuals are exactly what automatic
differentiation wants. There is no case analysis on which object type you have,
no coordinate-frame bookkeeping, and no branching on tensor values, so the
whole expression is a straight-line program over `Nx` and it differentiates and
compiles ([[traced]]).

```ocaml
open Geom

let residual p =
  let x = Cga.point p in
  let pi =
    Cga.plane ~normal:(Nx.create Nx.float32 [|3|] [|0.;0.;1.|])
      ~offset:(Nx.scalar Nx.float32 1.)
  in
  Cga.scalar_prod x pi

let value, grad = Rune.value_and_grad' residual
                    (Nx.create Nx.float32 [|3|] [|0.; 0.; 2.|])
(* value 1., grad [|0.; 0.; 1.|] : the signed distance and its gradient *)
```

That example is a signed point-to-plane distance, and its gradient is the plane
normal; that is not a coincidence, it is the reason these residuals are good
objectives ([[ga-residuals]]). The price is [[numerics|numerical care]]:
projective representations carry a scale, and a residual that is polynomial in
the raw coefficients is not automatically normalised.
