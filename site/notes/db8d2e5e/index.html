The adjoint turns a sandwich into a matrix

#ga/motion

For a fixed versor , the sandwich is linear in , so it is a matrix in the blade basis. That matrix is the adjoint. For the dense algebra it is ; for a motor it is the usual adjoint of SE(3) acting on twists.

open Geom
let v3 x y z = Nx.create Nx.float32 [| 3 |] [| x; y; z |] in
let sc x = Nx.scalar Nx.float32 x in

let m = Motor.rotate ~axis:(v3 0. 0. 1.) ~angle:(sc (Float.pi /. 2.)) in
Nx.shape (Motor.adjoint m)          (* [|6; 6|] *)
Nx.shape (Cga.adjoint (Cga.of_motor m))  (* [|32; 32|] *)

Two uses. The first is composition of many motions applied to many points: a matrix times a batch of 32-vectors is one dot, which is often faster than a batched sandwich. The second is the Lie-theoretic one: the adjoint carries twists (and, dually, wrenches) between frames, and the adjoint of a composition is the product of the adjoints, .

The dense adjoint is also the cleanest test of the sandwich implementation: apply the sandwich to each basis blade, stack the results, and compare with the matrix. That is a cheap property test, and it catches sign errors that a hand-written example would miss (Numerical cautions in a projective algebra).
